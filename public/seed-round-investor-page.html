What SHOGI does differently

National-security-grade authority, outside the autonomous system’s control.

SHOGI combines two inputs. Policy defines what the user expects the agent to do. STPA-Sec constraints define what the agent is not permitted to do. Together they are encoded as an ordered identity inside a SHOGI Identity-Based Encryption (IBE) key, built on post-quantum Learning With Errors (LWE) cryptography, with signcryption built in.

SHOGI builds its own agents and equips the systems they act on with SHOGI enforcement software: servers, satellites, drones, critical infrastructure, smart cities, industrial IoT, and physical AI. The agent’s key sets the outer limits: an action outside policy, or against STPA-Sec constraints, is outside what the key can authorize. Within those limits, every critical action needs a one-time permit for that exact action, approved by a quorum. On the protected system, a designated driver that runs no AI holds the real credentials, checks the permit, performs the action once and signs a receipt. The agent never holds the power to act alone.

STPA-Sec (System-Theoretic Process Analysis for Security) was created by SHOGI advisor and STPA-Sec Integration Lead Dr. William “Dollar” Young, a retired USAF Colonel. It identifies the losses a mission cannot accept and derives the constraints that forbid the actions leading to them. It is used at U.S. Cyber Command and in security engineering for the U.S. Air Force’s Sentinel intercontinental ballistic missile program, and Dollar’s work has contributed to NSA-sponsored research on AI mission assurance that incorporates it. The supporting Syracuse University paper is linked from slide 2 of the investor deck.

Dollar established the 350th Spectrum Warfare Wing. He participates in the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency’s Information Science and Technology Study Group and is currently developing the protection plan for the Pentagon’s AI-assisted classification-management pilot.

The SHOGI team has reviewed twenty existing platforms to identify possible design partners, including multiple companies that also use cryptography. These companies primarily authenticate, meaning a signed token says who the agent is and what it may access. SHOGI adds a signed, one-time permit that proves that this exact action was approved by the right parties, and the system that acts checks it before anything happens. Every identity, across groups and sub-agents, is tied back to the human who created it. Ordered identities and signcryption, a single process that performs both encryption and authentication, make that possible. A few platforms have started to sign approvals. In our review, none yet does it in production, and none combines it with rules derived from safety analysis and post-quantum cryptography.

Even if an autonomous system’s decision-making is manipulated, protected actions still require valid cryptographic authority, enforced by the system being acted on rather than by the agent. No valid authority means no execution.

Why now

Regulators and buyers are already moving.

Autonomous AI is moving into consequential work, and trust at that level has to be enforced, not only promised. Every AI provider, hyperscaler, systems integrator, security platform, industrial technology leader, and chipmaker deploying autonomous systems will need an authority layer. Each will have to build it or acquire it.

In May 2026, NSA, CISA and their Five Eyes partners recommended human approval before high-impact agent actions. In October, Cisco’s president called AI agent safety “the issue of our time”. Buyers are consolidating the category: Cisco agreed in May to buy Astrix for a reported $400 million, Rubrik bought Strata in June, and Cyera completed its $1 billion purchase of Oasis Security in September. And the elliptic-curve cryptography behind most of today’s signatures faces both quantum computers and new research questioning its foundations. SHOGI’s cryptography is lattice-based from the start.
